Week 2 Reflection

This week, in my first workshop, I built my own web page using HTML and CSS, and published it online with GitHub Pages. Before this, I had only ever used websites — I had never thought about how they are made.

Two things surprised me. First, a web page is really two files working together: HTML gives the page its structure, and CSS gives it its style. Changing one line of CSS can completely change how the page looks. Second, publishing is not the same as saving. My page was not visible to others until I set up GitHub Pages correctly.

I also made a mistake that taught me something useful. When my website first showed a 404 error, I spent a long time thinking my account had been blocked. In fact, the problem was much simpler — I had typed the wrong address. This reminded me that, in digital work, checking the small details carefully is as important as understanding the big ideas.

This task connected to our reading on algorithms. Building a page myself made me realise that the internet is not a neutral space — it is made of choices, and now I am starting to understand how those choices are made.

Week 3 Reflection: Web Scraping

This week I used the Web Scraper browser extension to build my first dataset, by scraping the BBC iPlayer homepage. I created a sitemap, defined a selector targeting the "New & Trending" section, and extracted the titles of five programmes.

The process taught me two things. First, a website is not a "given" object but an assembly of code. Scraping works by reading that code, and my selector had to target the exact HTML container in order to return clean data — a small mistake in the selector and I collected a cookie banner instead of programmes.

Second, and more importantly, I experienced the limitation the workshop kept returning to: scraping can only harvest what the code defines. I could collect the title of a programme, but the tool told me nothing about why the BBC chose to promote it, or how audiences experience it. The data arrived without context, and the meaning still has to come from human interpretation.

A technical struggle was also instructive. My scraper's export options would only flatten all the titles into one cell, or spread them across separate columns, rather than listing them one per row. Getting clean, analysable data meant knowing how to work around the tool's limits — which is itself a research skill, and a reminder that "raw data" is never quite raw.

Week 4 Reflection: Making a Dataset

Our task was to work as a small data analysis company and help a university understand how generative AI affects academic integrity. That question was far too broad to research in a week, so our group narrowed it to something we could actually check: how sixteen UK universities describe the rules students must follow. No survey and no interviews were allowed, so the only source was public policy text — which has the advantage that a reader can open every single cell and check it.

The most useful moment came from a mistake. Our first eight institutions were all Russell Group, and the finding looked clean: not one of them used AI detection software. At the time it read like an industry fact. It was a subset fact. When we added eight post-92 and specialist institutions, Leeds Beckett stated that it had introduced Turnitin's AI detection tool in 2023/24, and the conclusion reversed. What I take from this is that the boundary of a sample is the boundary of a claim: an argument can be internally correct and still only be about the cases you happened to choose.

The hardest step was turning a sentence of policy into a single number. Cardiff Met writes that detection methods "may be utilised", which is neither a use nor a refusal. Our coding frame had no category for "unclear", so the cell became a 0. That is not a weakness in the university; it is a weakness in our frame, and I only noticed it because we argued about that one cell three times.

I also had to accept what the method could not show. Coding silence as 0 leaves 53 per cent of the non-Russell-Group cells empty, but that figure can only be read as "the public text does not say", never as "the university does not care". Keeping those two sentences apart is part of the research rather than a disclaimer added at the end — and it is why the dataset answers what institutions state, not what students experience.

Week 5 Reflection: Visualising Data

What I learned this week is that a chart is not a picture of data. It is an argument about what counts. We visualised the same sixteen institutions we had coded the week before, and every decision about form also decided what the audience was able to conclude.

The first decision was to keep the empty cells. In our heatmap a grey square means that the public text records nothing at that intersection — a null, not a zero. The easy option was to drop those rows so that the chart looked complete. We kept them, and the argument changed: an absence in a policy document is evidence about the institution, not a hole in our dataset. The cost is honest too, because coding a null as 0 puts "not regulated" and "regulated but never written down" into the same cell.

The second decision concerned grouping. With a Russell-Group-only sample, a stacked bar split by sector told a clean story: no detection anywhere. The grouping did not describe that story, it produced it. Sort the same rows alphabetically instead and the reversal disappears. I had assumed that categories were neutral containers for data; they are part of the claim.

The third decision was the caption. One of our charts shows 25 per cent against 53 per cent, which reads as a governance gap. It is a publication gap, because our data only records what institutions put on the open web. Writing "public text" into the title is an ethical decision rather than a wording preference, and I now think of captions as part of the analysis rather than decoration on top of it.

We also built a ranking of universities by strictness and then deleted it. The data could not support a ranking, and presenting one would have implied a judgement we had no evidence for. Removing something is a design choice as well.
